Remote work is often described as either a perfect freedom or a slow disaster, and after three years of it I think both descriptions are too simple. Working from home removed my commute, which gave me almost an hour every day for reading and cooking. I also control my schedule, so I can stop when a problem is solved instead of when a clock reaches five. These are real improvements, and I would not give them up easily.

The costs are less obvious at the beginning. Meetings move online and multiply, because talking to a colleague now requires an invitation. New employees miss the small conversations that teach them how the team really works, and I have watched talented people struggle for months without anyone noticing. Silence at home can also turn into isolation, especially in winter.

A sensible arrangement probably mixes the two. Offices are useful for planning, training and social contact, while home is better for concentrated work. Companies that treat remote work as a test of trust usually do better than those that install monitoring software. The question is not where people work, but whether the work is clear, supported and fairly judged.
